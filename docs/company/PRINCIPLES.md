# Company Operating Principles

- **Status:** Active company doctrine
- **Owners:** Juan C Gonzalez and Shawn Sebastian Punch
- **Adopted:** 26 October 2026
- **Review cadence:** At least annually and whenever conduct exposes a gap

## Purpose

These principles describe how we intend to build, sell, and operate a
cybersecurity company. They apply to founder decisions, product design,
customer work, research, partnerships, hiring, and future leadership. They do
not replace law, contractual duties, professional standards, or the specific
security boundaries documented elsewhere in this repository.

The structure draws inspiration from the directness and urgency of the ten
principles circulated inside Nike in 1977. The historical headings are shown
below so the source of each adaptation remains visible. Our adopted principles,
explanations, and operating tests are original company policy. Nike's memo
reflected a different company and era; we do not adopt language that excuses
unlawful conduct, treats business as war, or values speed above safety. Nike is
not affiliated with or endorsing this company. [S26, S27]

Some transcriptions of the historical third principle include a directive to
break rules and fight the law. That directive is not company policy. Security
work requires lawful authorization, defensible methods, customer protection,
and honest reporting. Results and process therefore carry equal weight here.

## The ten principles

### 1. Change is the environment we serve

Threats, systems, businesses, and customer needs keep changing. We design for
adaptation without changing claims, controls, or scope casually. We revise a
decision when evidence changes, and we preserve the reason for the revision.

**Operating test:** What changed, what evidence supports it, and what must we
revalidate?

### 2. Act with initiative

We raise risks early, run the next safe experiment, and take ownership of
problems within our authority. Urgency means reducing avoidable delay. It does
not mean bypassing customer approval, security review, or operational safety.

**Operating test:** What useful, authorized action can we complete now?

### 3. Outcomes and methods both matter

A useful result does not excuse an unsafe, deceptive, or unlawful process. We
measure whether the customer received a correct and understandable outcome,
and whether we produced it through authorized, reproducible, and ethical
methods.

**Operating test:** Would we defend both the result and the method to the
customer, a reviewer, and each other?

### 4. Treat the mission as consequential

Security work can affect livelihoods, operations, privacy, and public trust.
We bring discipline and persistence to that responsibility without using fear
to sell or overstating what our evidence proves.

**Operating test:** Have we explained the real consequence and the limits of
our conclusion in plain language?

### 5. Question assumptions and honor commitments

We verify inputs, test important beliefs, document promises, and surface
uncertainty. We challenge one another respectfully and never make a customer,
partner, or teammate carry an unstated assumption.

**Operating test:** Which assumption could change this decision, and who owns
verifying it?

### 6. Use resources deliberately

We prefer focused experiments, simple architecture, customer-controlled data,
and tools that fit the problem. We do not build infrastructure, integrations,
or features merely to appear mature. Lightweight must still be dependable.

**Operating test:** Is this the smallest responsible investment that can
produce trustworthy evidence?

### 7. Finish the outcome

Activity is not completion. Work is complete when its acceptance criteria are
met, material limits are recorded, the result is understandable, and the next
owner can act. A finding without review or a change without verification is
unfinished.

**Operating test:** Can the intended person use and verify what we delivered?

### 8. Guard the company against predictable failure

We watch for bureaucracy, ego, hidden incentives, unmanaged fatigue,
unfocused work, and unexamined weaknesses. We limit work in progress, invite
dissent, separate evidence from seniority, and protect time for correction.

**Operating test:** Are we serving the mission, or protecting a plan, title,
feature, or personal preference?

### 9. Expect difficult work and communicate clearly

Building a trustworthy security company will involve incomplete data, failed
experiments, uncomfortable feedback, and changing plans. Internal difficulty
must never become confusing customer output, concealed risk, or careless
operations.

**Operating test:** What is unresolved, who needs to know, and what is the
clearest responsible next step?

### 10. Earn durable business by doing right

We seek revenue by solving a real problem well, protecting customer trust, and
keeping our promises. We refuse growth that depends on misleading claims,
unsafe access, hidden data use, manufactured urgency, or customer lock-in.
Financial discipline matters because it enables the mission to continue.

**Operating test:** Would this decision still be right if the customer could
see the full reasoning and incentives behind it?

## How we use the principles

- Important proposals identify the principle or principles they advance and
  any tension they create.
- Product and security reviews apply the operating tests, not only the titles.
- Customer-facing claims remain bounded by evidence, scope, and stated limits.
- Anyone working with the company may raise a concern without retaliation.
- Violations are recorded, corrected, and used to improve the relevant
  process. Repeated violations require an explicit founder response.
- The founders review this doctrine together at least annually. A change is
  recorded in the decision log rather than silently editing the standard.

These principles define expected judgment; they are not marketing copy. We
will be judged by repeated conduct, not by the existence of this document.
